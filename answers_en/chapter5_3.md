**1. Why Do We Need to Train a Reward Model?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

The Reward Model (RM) is the critical bridge connecting "human preferences" with "policy optimization" in RLHF. Its necessity is reflected in the following aspects:

**1. Humans cannot provide real-time feedback**

RL training requires a reward signal for **every generated response** (rollout). If human annotation were required each time, a single round of training would involve scoring hundreds of thousands of responses—which is completely infeasible in terms of time and labor. The reward model "crystallizes" human preferences into an automatically callable scoring function through "train once, infer infinitely."

**2. Rewards need to be differentiable for gradient optimization**

Policy gradient methods such as PPO require reward signals to calculate the advantage function, indirectly driving parameter updates. A reward model implemented as a neural network outputs continuous scalar scores and seamlessly integrates with the overall training workflow.

**3. Generalizing preferences to unseen responses**

Human annotated data covers only a fraction of (prompt, response) pairs, whereas during RL training the model generates a vast number of **new responses never seen in the annotated data**. By learning the "underlying patterns of preferences" from annotated data, the reward model can assign reasonable scores to these novel responses, achieving generalization of preferences.

**4. Stabilizing reward signals**

Using human preference votes directly as rewards would introduce significant noise due to inter-annotator variance. A trained reward model outputs consistent scores for identical inputs, reducing variance during RL training.

In short, the reward model serves as a **differentiable, reusable, and generalizable digital proxy for human preferences**, enabling large-scale RL training.

</details>

**2. How Is the Reward Model Trained?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

The training workflow for a Reward Model (RM) includes:
1. **Collecting Preference Data**: Use an initial model to generate multiple responses, which human annotators compare or rate.
2. **Pairwise Contrastive Training**: The reward model outputs a scalar score, and is trained by comparing scores across different responses.
3. **Model Architecture**: Typically constructed by appending a “Reward Head” onto a pre-trained language model.
4. **Usage Scenario**: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward model scores generated responses, serving as reward signals to optimize the policy model.

</details>

**3. Architecture Selection for Reward Models**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![Reward Model Architecture with Value Head](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig14_architecture_selection_for_rew.png)




Reward models typically **directly reuse the same (or similar) pre-trained Transformer architecture as the LLM to be optimized**. Common practices include:

- Initializing from the SFT model (or a pre-trained base of similar scale), **replacing/removing the language model head (LM head)**, and attaching a **linear layer outputting a scalar** at the final layer, converting the task from "predicting the next token" to "predicting a scalar score for the overall response."
- Typically, the hidden state of the final token (or EOS token) in the sequence is passed through a linear projection to obtain the reward value.

**Relationship with the Final Optimized LLM**:
- Both share a similar foundation of language understanding capabilities (often using the same tokenizer and architecture), enabling the RM to accurately comprehend the semantics of content generated by the policy model.
- The RM is **frozen** during the RL stage, acting as part of the environment to provide reward signals without participating in gradient updates.
- The scale of the RM is generally no smaller than that of the policy model (sometimes larger), because discrimination (distinguishing good from bad) can be as challenging as generation itself.

</details>

**4. How Is the Reward Model Trained in RLHF?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

The reward model is trained on human preference data:
1. For a given prompt, prompt the model to generate two or more responses
2. Human annotators rank these responses by preference (selecting the better one)
3. Convert the rankings into preference pairs (chosen, rejected), and model preference probabilities using the Bradley-Terry model
4. Training objective: Maximize the probability that the score of the chosen response is higher than that of the rejected response
Once trained, the reward model can score any response, providing reward signals for subsequent PPO training.

</details>

**5. During RLHF Training, Are the Reward Model and LLM Trained Simultaneously or Sequentially? How Was the RM Trained in the InstructGPT Paper?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![Sequential Training and Integration of RM and Actor](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig15_during_rlhf_training_are_the_r.png)




**Training Order of the Reward Model and LLM (Policy Model)**:

Overall, they are trained **sequentially** across multiple stages:

1. **Stage 1: SFT**: Perform supervised fine-tuning on the pre-trained model using human-annotated high-quality demonstration data to obtain the SFT model;
2. **Stage 2: Reward Model (RM) Training**: Train an independent reward model using human preference rankings over model outputs. RM training in this stage is **decoupled** from RL training of the policy model—the RM is trained separately first and then fixed;
3. **Stage 3: RL Fine-Tuning (PPO)**: Using the SFT model as initialization (and also as the reference model), use the **already trained and parameter-frozen** RM to provide reward signals for PPO training, optimizing the policy model via reinforcement learning.

During PPO training, **the RM itself is no longer updated** (remaining frozen); only the policy model (actor) and value model (critic) undergo training. Therefore, strictly speaking, "RM training" and "RL training of the policy model" are two **sequential, non-overlapping** stages rather than "simultaneous training."

**RM Training Method in InstructGPT**:

1. **Data Collection**: For a given prompt, have the model (SFT model) generate **multiple (e.g., 4--9) candidate responses**, which human annotators rank from best to worst;

2. **Training Objective—Pairwise Comparison**: Convert ranking results into pairwise comparison data; specifically, for every pair ($y_w$, $y_l$) in the ranking where $y_w$ is preferred over $y_l$, construct a training sample;

3. **Loss Function (Based on the Bradley-Terry Model)**:

\[
loss(\theta) = -E[log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l))]
\]

where $r_\theta(x,y)$ is the scalar score output by the reward model for (prompt $x$, response $y$), and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. This loss encourages the model to assign higher scores to "preferred responses" than to "dispreferred responses"; the larger the difference, the closer $\sigma$ is to 1, and the lower the loss.

1. **Model Architecture**: The RM is typically based on the same/similar pre-trained architecture as the policy model, replacing the language modeling head at the final layer with a **scalar-outputting linear layer** (outputting a single value at the sequence end position), fine-tuned on preference data.

2. **Handling Multiple Comparison Pairs per Prompt**: Since $K$ responses under a single prompt yield $\binom{K}{2}$ comparison pairs, the InstructGPT paper **treats all comparison pairs from the same prompt as a single "sample group" within a batch** (rather than scattering individual comparison pairs across different batches). This approach is computationally more efficient and has been found to help mitigate overfitting.

**Summary**: The RM and policy model are **trained sequentially** (the RM is trained and frozen before being used for PPO training of the policy model), not simultaneously optimized. In InstructGPT, the RM is trained on "human rankings over multiple candidate responses" via a **pairwise comparison Bradley-Terry loss (log-sigmoid of reward difference)**.

</details>

**6. In the RM Training Stage, We Typically Collect Pairwise Comparison Data Rather Than Having Human Annotators Give an Absolute Score Directly to Each Response. What Do You Think Are the Main Advantages and Potential Disadvantages of This Approach?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

During reward model (RM) training, adopting pairwise comparison rather than absolute scoring is the standard industry practice, backed by profound cognitive science and practical considerations.

Main Advantages:

1. Reduces cognitive load and improves annotation consistency: Having humans choose "which is better" among options is far easier and more intuitive than assigning a precise absolute score (e.g., 1 to 10) to a single response. Different annotators may have drastically different definitions of a "7/10," but easily reach consensus on "A is better than B," which greatly enhances inter-rater agreement.
2. Provides finer-grained signals: Comparison data can capture subtle preference differences. Two responses might both be rated as "good" in absolute terms (e.g., both 8/10), but comparison data can explicitly indicate that one is "slightly better" than the other. This relative signal is crucial for the model to learn fine-grained preferences.
3. Normalizes data distribution: Absolute scores are easily affected by annotators' personal moods, scoring scales, fatigue, and other factors, leading to uneven score distributions or biases. Comparison data naturally converts the problem into a standardized binary classification or ranking task, where the model only needs to learn relative relationships and is insensitive to absolute scale.

Potential Disadvantages:

1. Lower potential data efficiency: Each comparison yields only about 1 bit of information ($A > B$ or $B > A$). Fully ranking $K$ responses requires $O(K^2)$ comparisons, whereas absolute scoring requires only $K$ evaluations. This implies that achieving an equivalent amount of information may require more annotation effort.
2. Susceptibility to intransitivity: Human preferences sometimes violate transitivity, resulting in cyclic preferences such as "A is better than B, B is better than C, but C is better than A." This introduces noise and contradictory training signals to the reward model.
3. Incomplete information: Comparison data only reveals relative superiority/inferiority without specifying "how much better" or "how much worse." The gap between two responses could be negligible or vast, but pairwise comparisons cannot directly reflect the magnitude of this difference.

</details>

**7. Reward Model Design Is Crucial. How Is Its Model Architecture Typically Chosen? What Is Its Relationship with the Final LLM We Aim to Optimize? What Is the Commonly Used Loss Function When Training the Reward Model? Please Explain the Mathematical Principles Behind It (e.g., in Combination with the Bradley-Terry Model).**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![Bradley-Terry Preference Loss Calculation for RM Training](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig16_reward_model_design_is_crucial.png)




Model Architecture Selection: The architecture of the reward model (RM) is typically chosen to be identical or very similar to the LLM being optimized, with two key differences:

1. The initialization weights of the RM usually come from the SFT model trained in the first stage. This ensures that the RM possesses a strong foundational understanding of instructions and language style.
2. The final layer of the RM (typically the softmax layer for next-token prediction) is replaced with a Regression Head, usually a linear layer, which outputs a scalar representing the reward score.

Relationship with the Final LLM: The RM serves as a proxy for the utility function of the final LLM. It acts as a simulator of human preferences within the RLHF pipeline. The goal of the final LLM (the policy) is to generate responses that yield high scores from this RM. Consequently, the quality of the RM directly determines the alignment ceiling of the final LLM. If the RM contains flaws or biases, the LLM will engage in "reward hacking" during optimization, exploiting these defects to obtain high scores rather than generating responses that humans genuinely prefer.

Commonly Used Loss Function: The most commonly used loss function in RM training is the Pairwise Ranking Loss. Its goal is to ensure that, for any given prompt, the RM assigns a higher score $r(y_w)$ to the "winning response" ($y_w$) than the score $r(y_l)$ assigned to the "losing response" ($y_l$).

Mathematical Principles (in combination with the Bradley-Terry Model): The Bradley-Terry model describes the probability of outcomes in pairwise comparisons. It assumes that each entity (in this context, each response) possesses a latent "strength" score (i.e., the reward score $r$). The probability $P(y_w > y_l)$ that response $y_w$ is preferred over $y_l$ is modeled using a logistic function (i.e., the sigmoid function $\sigma$):

\[P(y_w > y_l | x) = \sigma(r(y_w | x) - r(y_l | x))\]

where $x$ is the prompt and $r(y|x)$ is the score output by the RM. Intuitively, this formula indicates that a larger gap between the reward scores of two responses corresponds to higher confidence that one is superior to the other.

During training, our objective is to maximize the log-likelihood of the observed human preference data. For a preference pair $(y_w, y_l)$, we aim to maximize the log of $P(y_w > y_l)$. Thus, the loss function is its negative log-likelihood:

\[Loss = -\log(P(y_w > y_l | x)) = -\log(\sigma(r(y_w | x) - r(y_l | x)))\]

This loss function penalizes cases where the RM assigns incorrect scores (i.e., $r(y_l) > r(y_w)$) and drives the RM to learn a scoring function that accurately reflects human preference rankings.

</details>

**8. When Training an RM, Whether in InstructGPT or DPO, Why Are the Log and Sigmoid Functions Used in the Loss? Why Not Simply Subtract the Rewards Directly?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

**Why Sigmoid and Log Are Used (i.e., the Bradley-Terry Model)**:

1. **Converting "Score Differences" into "Preference Probabilities"—Modeling the Stochasticity of Human Preferences**:

Human preference judgments inherently possess stochasticity and uncertainty—given two responses A and B, even if A is objectively slightly better than B, annotators will not select A 100% of the time (e.g., 90% of annotators choose A and 10% choose B, especially when their quality is close). The Bradley-Terry model assumes:

\[
P(A  preferred over  B) = \sigma(r(A) - r(B)) = 1 / (1 + exp(-(r(A) - r(B))))
\]

That is, "the probability of A being preferred" is the result of mapping the reward difference between the two through sigmoid—**the larger the difference, the closer the preferred probability of A is to 1; when the difference is 0, the preference probability is 0.5 (complete uncertainty); when the difference is negative, the probability is less than 0.5**. This modeling naturally converts a "continuous reward difference" into a "probability in the range [0,1]," matching the true nature of human annotated data (a discrete choice with uncertainty).

1. **Log Is Used to Convert "Maximizing Probability" into an Optimizable Loss (Maximum Likelihood Estimation)**:

Given the annotation data "A is preferred over B," we want the model's output $P(A  preferred over  B)$ to be as close to 1 as possible (i.e., the model should "believe" the annotator's choice). This is a standard **maximum likelihood estimation** problem: maximizing log $P(A  preferred over  B) = log  \sigma(r(A) - r(B))$, and the corresponding loss is its negative $-log  \sigma(r(A) - r(B))$. This aligns exactly with the loss function of logistic regression—essentially modeling "which response is better" as a **binary classification problem**, where the classification "logit" is the difference between the two rewards.

**Why Rewards Cannot Be Subtracted Directly (e.g., Minimizing $-(r(A) - r(B))$ or Similar Margin Losses)**:

1. **Lack of Probabilistic Interpretation and Proper "Saturation" Mechanism**: If $-(r(A) - r(B))$ were used directly as the loss (aiming to maximize the difference indefinitely), this loss **has no upper bound and no natural "saturation point"**—the model would be driven to push $r(A) - r(B)$ to infinity, potentially causing reward values to grow uncontrollably in scale and destabilizing training. In contrast, with the sigmoid+log form of loss, when $r(A) - r(B)$ is already sufficiently large ($\sigma(\cdot)$ close to 1), the loss approaches 0 and **gradients naturally decay (saturation)**, avoiding infinite divergence of reward differences. This represents a far more reasonable and stable optimization target.

2. **Alignment with the "Probabilistic" Nature of Human Annotation**: Using reward differences directly as the optimization target assumes that "annotator choices are absolute and noise-free." However, actual annotation data contains many "corner cases" (where response quality is very close and annotations exhibit stochasticity). The Bradley-Terry loss incorporates this uncertainty into the model via sigmoid, ensuring that for "hard-to-distinguish" pairs, the model is not forced to generate an artificially massive reward gap (since the gradient of sigmoid near 0 is smooth).

3. **Numerical Stability and Gradient Properties**: The log-sigmoid loss (equivalent to the softplus function: $-log  \sigma(x) = log(1 + \exp(-x)) = softplus(-x)$) possesses excellent numerical stability and smooth gradient characteristics, making it a widely used and well-validated loss formulation in machine learning (sharing its roots with logistic regression and binary cross-entropy).

4. **Theoretical Foundation (Consistent with Optimal Policy Derivations in RL)**: The Bradley-Terry model shares a tight mathematical connection with the theoretical framework of RLHF (where the reward function corresponds to the log-probability ratio under an optimal policy, which serves as the foundation for DPO derivation), making the overall RLHF/DPO framework theoretically self-consistent.

**Summary**: The core reason for using sigmoid+log (i.e., Bradley-Terry loss) is to **transform the difference in reward values between two responses into an optimization objective that matches the true nature of human annotation data (probabilistic choices with uncertainty)**. This formulation offers a natural probabilistic interpretation, favorable gradient saturation properties, and numerical stability. Directly subtracting reward values lacks these properties, leading to an unbounded training target that fails to account for the probabilistic nature of annotations, making it an inappropriate loss design.

</details>

**9. Pairwise Comparison vs. Absolute Scoring**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

**Advantages**:
- **Higher Consistency**: Relative human judgments on "which is better" are more stable and reliable than absolute scores, exhibiting smaller standard deviation across different annotators.
- **Lower Annotation Cost and Cognitive Load**: Comparing two options is far easier than assigning a precise 1--10 score to a single response.
- **Naturally Fits the Bradley-Terry Model**: Pairwise preference data can be directly applied to classic preference modeling frameworks with mature theoretical foundations.

**Potential Disadvantages**:
- **Lower Information Density**: A single comparison yields only about 1 bit of information (whether A is better or B is better), requiring a large number of comparisons to accurately characterize preference distributions.
- **Transitivity Assumption May Fail**: Human preferences may exhibit non-transitivity ($A > B, B > C$, but $C > A$), whereas the Bradley-Terry model assumes a globally consistent latent score.
- **Difficulty in Expressing "Absolute Quality"**: For instance, if both responses are very poor, the comparison only informs the model that "A is slightly better than B," but cannot inform the model that "both are failing."

</details>

**10. Can You Introduce the BT Model (Bradley-Terry) in DPO?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

The Bradley-Terry (BT) model serves as the mathematical foundation for both DPO and reward model training. It is a statistical framework specifically designed for modeling preference probabilities in **pairwise comparisons**.

**Core Assumption**: Each candidate (here, each response $y$) possesses a latent "strength score" (i.e., reward $r(x,y)$). When comparing two responses $y_w$ (preferred) and $y_l$ (dispreferred), the probability of humans choosing $y_w$ is modeled by:
\[P(y_w \succ y_l | x) = \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)) = \frac{e^{r(x,y_w)}}{e^{r(x,y_w)} + e^{r(x,y_l)}}\]
where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. The larger the difference between the reward scores of the two responses, the more confidently humans prefer the higher-reward response.

**Application in Reward Model Training**:

Maximize the log-likelihood of the observed preference data, with the loss function:
\[\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}[\log\sigma(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l))]\]
Train the reward model $r_\phi$ such that chosen responses consistently receive higher scores than rejected responses.

**Application in DPO**:

DPO further derives that the optimal RLHF policy can express rewards using the log-probability ratio of $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{ref}$: $r(x,y) = \beta \log(\pi_\theta(y|x)/\pi_{ref}(y|x)) + const$. Substituting this into the BT model yields the direct loss function of DPO, completely bypassing explicit reward modeling.

The core contribution of the BT model is providing a **concise and trainable preference probability model**, converting the subjective judgment of "which response humans prefer" into an optimizable mathematical objective.

</details>

**11. Advantages and Disadvantages of the BT Model?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

**Advantages of the Bradley-Terry Model**:
- **Mathematically Concise and Elegant**: Fully models pairwise comparison probabilities through a single real-valued "strength score", featuring a simple form with a closed log-likelihood loss that is easy to optimize using standard gradient descent;
- **Consistency Guarantee (Transitivity)**: The BT model satisfies transitivity—if $A > B$ and $B > C$, then $A > C$ (guaranteed by the underlying score magnitudes), aligning with human preference logic;
- **Efficient Utilization of Pairwise Data**: Each pair of comparison data (chosen, rejected) provides a clear training signal without requiring absolute scores, reducing annotation difficulty;
- **Naturally Compatible with LLM Training Frameworks**: The reward model simply adds a linear head on top of the pretrained language model, allowing BT loss gradients to be backpropagated efficiently.

**Disadvantages of the Bradley-Terry Model**:
- **Assumes Preferences are Transitive (Transitivity Assumption)**: Actual human preferences sometimes fail to satisfy transitivity (e.g., cyclic preferences $A > B$, $B > C$, but $C > A$). In such scenarios, the BT model's assumption is violated, generating contradictory training signals;
- **Inability to Model Preference Magnitude**: The BT model only models "which is better", not "by how much". Response pairs with subtle differences versus vast gulfs share the same data format, despite conveying drastically different amounts of information;
- **Sensitive to Noisy Annotations**: When annotator preference judgments contain noise or inconsistency, the BT loss forcibly fits this noise;
- **One Root Cause of Reward Hacking**: As a neural network, the RM gives unreliable scores on out-of-distribution samples, leading PPO training to exploit "loopholes" in the reward model.

</details>

**12. What Are the Issues with the BT Model (Training Form of DPO and RM)?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

1. **Intransitivity of Preferences**: The BT model assumes preference transitivity, but human preference judgments often do not satisfy this property.
2. **Limitations of the Independence Assumption**: The BT model assumes each pairwise comparison is independent, whereas in practice human preference judgments usually depend on context.
3. **Scoring Scale Issue**: The model simplifies preferences into binary comparisons, making it difficult to capture magnitude differences.
4. **Model Collapse**: The model tends to only generate positive examples, with the probability of negative examples collapsing to zero.

</details>

**14. Why Does PPO Require a Critic Model in RLHF Instead of Using the Reward Model Directly?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![Roles of Critic Model and Reward Model in PPO](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig17_why_does_ppo_require_a_critic.png)




The responsibilities of the Reward Model and the Critic are completely different and non-interchangeable; understanding their distinction is key to understanding the PPO architecture.

**Responsibilities of the Reward Model**: Evaluates the final quality **after completing the entire response**. It receives (prompt, full response) as input and outputs a scalar score, called only once at the end of sequence generation to give a signal of "how good this response is overall".

**Responsibilities of the Critic**: Estimates **at each token generation step** "the expected future cumulative reward obtained by continuing generation from the current state under the current policy"—that is, the state-value function $V(s_t)$. The Critic needs to provide an estimate at every intermediate position of the sequence, not just at the end.

**Why the Reward Model Cannot Replace the Critic**:
- **Temporal Timing Issue**: The Reward Model cannot provide meaningful scores before the sequence is complete; whereas PPO's GAE advantage estimation requires the value $V(s_t)$ at each token position $t$ to compute the TD error $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$;
- **Credit Assignment**: The Reward Model only provides the total reward at the end of the sequence, unable to distinguish which tokens in the sequence were "crucial good steps"; the Critic helps allocate the final reward back to each intermediate time step through step-by-step value estimation;
- **Different Training Objectives**: The Reward Model is trained on preference pair data to fit "overall response quality"; the Critic is trained via TD learning to fit "expected future return from the current state". Their inputs, outputs, and training signals are completely distinct.

GRPO completely avoids the need for a Critic by introducing intra-group normalization to obtain advantage estimates, representing a major engineering simplification over PPO.

</details>

**15. What is the Difference Between the Critic Model and the Reward Model?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

The Critic Model and Reward Model are two components in PPO training that are often confused, but their functions, training methods, and invocation timings are completely different.

**Reward Model**:
- **Function**: Evaluates the overall quality (how "good" it is) of a **complete response** relative to the prompt, outputting a scalar score;
- **Invocation Timing**: Called once **after** the entire response generation is completed, providing a sequence-level reward;
- **Training Method**: Trained on human preference data (chosen/rejected pairs) using the Bradley-Terry loss;
- **Status During Training**: Usually **frozen** during the PPO phase, without participating in gradient updates;
- **Role**: Serves as a "proxy for human preferences", acting as the source of alignment targets in RLHF.

**Critic Model**:
- **Function**: **At each token generation step**, estimates the expected cumulative future reward starting from the current state under the current policy (i.e., state-value function $V(s_t)$);
- **Invocation Timing**: Called **during** the generation of each token, providing dense intermediate value estimates;
- **Training Method**: Trained via TD learning (based on target values computed via GAE), updating alongside the Actor during the PPO phase;
- **Status During Training**: Continuously **updated** during the PPO phase, training synchronously with the Actor;
- **Role**: Provides an "advantage signal" for each token, helping the Actor assess the contribution of each action (token) to the final reward, solving the credit assignment problem.

**Simple Analogy**: The Reward Model is a "final examiner" (grading only after the essay is finished), while the Critic is a "real-time tutor" (offering guidance line by line during writing). GRPO simplifies this design by removing the Critic and replacing step-by-step value estimation with intra-group reward normalization.

</details>

**16. What is the Difference Between Critic and Reward in the PPO Algorithm?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

- **Different Sources**: The reward is provided directly by the environment, whereas the critic is learned by the model itself.
- **Different Time Horizons**: The reward is instantaneous and local; the critic (i.e., $V(s)$) is the weighted expectation of all future rewards.
- **Mathematical Relationship**: The reward serves as the supervision signal for the critic's learning. The objective of the critic is to make $V(s) \approx$ expected value of future rewards.
- **Different Roles in the Algorithm**: The reward is provided by the environment as the raw signal, while the critic is generated by the model as an estimator.

</details>
