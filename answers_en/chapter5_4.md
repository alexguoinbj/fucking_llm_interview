**1. What is the Basic Principle of Importance Sampling?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![Importance Sampling Weight Correction](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig18_what_is_the_basic_principle_of.png)




Importance Sampling (IS) is a statistical technique designed to solve the problem of "computing expectations under distribution $p$ when sampling is only possible from distribution $q$", serving as the mathematical foundation for the PPO algorithm.

**Core Idea**: By leveraging the definition of expectation and introducing a ratio weight $w(x) = p(x)/q(x)$, the expectation originally requiring samples from $p$ is converted into a weighted expectation over $q$:
\[\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\left[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} f(x)\right] = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}[w(x) \cdot f(x)]\]
where $w(x) = p(x)/q(x)$ is called the **Importance Weight**, used to "correct" distribution discrepancies between $q$ and $p$.

**Intuitive Understanding**: If $q$ has a higher probability in a region than $p$, that region is oversampled when sampling from $q$, requiring a small weight $w < 1$ to correct downward; if $q$ has a lower probability in a region than $p$, it is undersampled, requiring a large weight $w > 1$ to correct upward.

**Prerequisite**: Wherever $q(x) > 0$, $p(x)$ must also be $> 0$ (the support condition). Otherwise, regions with high probability under $p$ might never be sampled under $q$, leading to severe errors in expectation estimation.

**Application in PPO**: Allows using data collected by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_old}$ to update the new policy $\pi_\theta$, with the importance weight being $\pi_\theta(a|s)/\pi_{\theta_old}(a|s)$. The clipping mechanism in PPO is specifically designed to constrain this weight from straying too far from 1, thereby controlling variance.

</details>

**2. What is the Role of Importance Sampling?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

In reinforcement learning and LLM RL, importance sampling solves a core engineering problem: **how to reuse data generated by the old policy to update the new policy**, allowing on-policy methods to improve sample efficiency while maintaining theoretical correctness.

**1. Core Role in PPO**

After each rollout round, PPO performs **multiple epoch mini-batch updates** on the same batch of data (typically 2–4 epochs). After the first update, the policy parameters $\theta$ have already changed ($\theta \neq \theta_old$), but the data was still generated by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_old}$. The importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_old}(a|s)}$ is used to correct this distribution discrepancy, rendering "updating the new policy with old data" mathematically valid:
\[\mathcal{L}^{IS} = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a) \sim \pi_{\theta_old}}\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_old}(a|s)} \cdot A(s,a)\right]\]

**2. Enhancing Sample Efficiency**

Pure on-policy methods without importance sampling (such as REINFORCE) must discard all data and resample after every single update, which is extremely wasteful. Importance sampling allows each batch of rollout data to be reused multiple times, significantly reducing the frequency of online sampling (i.e., expensive LLM autoregressive generation).

**3. PPO's Clip Mechanism**

Since the variance of importance weights is positively correlated with the discrepancy between $p$ and $q$, PPO clips the ratio: $clip(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_old}, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)$, constraining the weights within a safe range. This controls variance while ensuring data reuse, preventing drastic policy shifts during updates.

**4. Manifestation in GRPO**

GRPO similarly utilizes the importance sampling ratio to compute its objective function, retaining PPO's clipping mechanism to ensure that policy drift does not exceed safe bounds during multi-step updates.

</details>

**3. Explain the Principle of Importance Sampling in the PPO Algorithm**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

The core principle of importance sampling in the PPO algorithm is using data from the old policy to estimate the expected return of the new policy, thereby avoiding the need to resample data after every policy update.


$$
	\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ f(a) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta (a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} f(a) \right]
$$


where $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}$ is the importance weight, used to correct differences in action distributions.

</details>

**4. Why Compute the Importance Parameter by Subtracting Logs and Exponentiating Rather Than Direct Division?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

1. **Numerical Stability**: Probability values can be extremely small; direct computation can lead to numerical underflow or overflow.
2. **Computational Efficiency**: log_softmax is a native operation, which is more efficient than calculating probabilities first and then taking logarithms.
3. **Gradient Computation**: Gradient calculation of log probabilities is numerically more stable.
4. **Mathematical Consistency**: Multiplication of probabilities converts to addition in log space.

</details>

**5. Derivation Process of Discrete KL Divergence**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

**1. Definition of KL Divergence**

KL Divergence (Kullback-Leibler Divergence) is defined as:

$$
	KL[q, p] = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q} \left[ \log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \right]
$$


**2. k1 Estimator**

k1 is the naive Monte Carlo estimator of KL divergence, based directly on its definition:

$$
	k1 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log \frac{q(x_i)}{p(x_i)}
$$


Properties: Unbiased, but with high variance.

**3. k2 Estimator**

k2 is an estimator based on second-order approximation, leveraging the local properties of KL divergence when $p \approx q$:

$$
	k2 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \right)^2
$$


Properties: Low variance, but biased.

**4. k3 Estimator**

k3 combines k1 with the concept of control variates:

$$
	k3 = (r - 1) - \log r, \quad r = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
$$


Properties: Unbiased, low variance, and good stability.

</details>

**6. Computation of Approximate KL Divergence**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

The full form of KL divergence may lack an analytical solution and incurs high computational complexity. Approximation methods need to balance bias and variance.

**Forward KL Divergence Approximation**:

$$
	Forward KL = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q} \left[ \log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \right] \approx -\log x
$$


**Reverse KL Divergence Approximation**:

$$
	Reverse KL = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p} \left[ \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \right] \approx x \log x
$$


Forward KL is extremely sensitive to low-probability events (mode-covering), whereas Reverse KL is more sensitive to high-probability events (mode-seeking). In large language models and generative tasks, Reverse KL is usually preferred.

</details>

**7. How is KL Divergence Computed in RL Algorithms like PPO?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

In algorithms such as PPO and GRPO, KL divergence is calculated as follows:

**PPO**: Typically uses $KL(\pi_\theta | \pi_{ref})$, which is Reverse KL.

**GRPO**: Likewise uses $KL(\pi_\theta | \pi_{ref})$.

Let $x = \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{old}(a|s)}$, then:
- Forward KL: $-\log x$
- Reverse KL: $x \log x$

Forward KL tends to make the model distribution cover all support points of the target distribution (mode-covering), while Reverse KL tends to concentrate the model distribution in high-probability regions of the target distribution (mode-seeking).

</details>

**8. How to Specifically Compute KL Divergence, and What Is the Relationship Between KL Divergence and Cross-Entropy?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![KL Divergence Constraint on Policy Drift](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig19_how_to_specifically_compute_kl.png)




**Computation of KL Divergence**:

For two probability distributions P and Q (defined on the same space), KL divergence (Kullback-Leibler Divergence) is defined as:

\[
KL(P||Q) = \Sigma_{x} P(x) · log(P(x)/Q(x)) = E_{x~P}[log P(x) - log Q(x)]
\]

(For continuous distributions, integration replaces summation).

**Computation in LLM Scenarios**: For policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and reference policy $\pi_{ref}$, given context s, over all possible next tokens in the vocabulary:

\[
KL(\pi_{\theta}(·|s)||\pi_{ref}(·|s)) = \Sigma_{token v} \pi_{\theta}(v|s) · log(\pi_{\theta}(v|s)/\pi_{ref}(v|s))
\]

Due to the large vocabulary size, direct summation over all tokens is computationally expensive. In practice, **Monte Carlo sampling estimation** is commonly used: for an actually sampled token $y_t$, log $\pi_{\theta}$($y_t$|$s_t$) - log $\pi_{ref}$($y_t$|$s_t$) is used as a (biased but simple) single-sample estimate of the KL divergence at that position. To obtain a lower-variance, (nearly) unbiased estimate, the aforementioned **k3 estimator** is frequently used:

\[
KL_estimate = \pi_{ref}(y_t|s_t)/\pi_{\theta}(y_t|s_t) - log(\pi_{ref}(y_t|s_t)/\pi_{\theta}(y_t|s_t)) - 1
\]

This expression leverages the property $e^x - x - 1 \ge 0$ (when $x = \log(\pi_{ref}/\pi_{\theta})$), guaranteeing that the estimated value is always non-negative (consistent with the non-negative property of KL divergence itself) with lower variance.

**Relationship Between KL Divergence and Cross-Entropy**:

Cross-Entropy is defined as:

\[
H(P,Q) = -\Sigma_{x} P(x)·log Q(x) = E_{x~P}[-log Q(x)]
\]

And the entropy of P is:

\[
H(P) = -\Sigma_{x} P(x)·log P(x) = E_{x~P}[-log P(x)]
\]

KL divergence can be expressed as the difference between cross-entropy and entropy:

\[
KL(P||Q) = \Sigma_{x} P(x)·log(P(x)/Q(x)) = \Sigma_{x} P(x)·log P(x) - \Sigma_{x} P(x)·log Q(x) = -H(P) + H(P,Q) = H(P,Q) - H(P)
\]

That is: **KL(P||Q) = Cross-entropy H(P,Q) - Entropy H(P)**

**Intuitive Understanding**:
- Cross-entropy $H(P,Q)$ represents "the average code length (in bits, if base 2 is used) required to encode data from distribution $P$ using distribution $Q$";
- Entropy $H(P)$ is "the average code length required to encode data from $P$ using distribution $P$ itself (the optimal code)", which represents the theoretical minimum;
- **KL divergence is "the 'extra' code length incurred by using $Q$ instead of $P$ to encode data"**, measuring the "discrepancy" between $Q$ and the true distribution $P$.

**Manifestation in Machine Learning Training**: In classification/language modeling tasks, for the common cross-entropy loss $-\sum y_{true} \cdot \log y_{pred}$, if $y_{true}$ is viewed as the true distribution $P$ (typically a one-hot distribution with entropy $H(P)=0$) and $y_{pred}$ is viewed as the model's predicted distribution $Q$, then **minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to minimizing KL(P||Q)** (since $H(P)=0$ is a constant and does not affect optimization). This is why cross-entropy loss and KL divergence are "equivalent" in classification optimization—although in the general case (when $P$ is not a degenerate one-hot distribution), the two differ by a constant term $H(P)$.

**Summary**: The formula for calculating KL divergence is $KL(P||Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[\log P(x) - \log Q(x)]$, where the k3 estimator is commonly used for low-variance estimation in LLM-RL; the relationship between KL divergence and cross-entropy is **KL(P||Q) = H(P,Q) - H(P)**, i.e., KL divergence is cross-entropy minus the entropy of the true distribution. When the true distribution is a deterministic distribution (with an entropy of 0, such as one-hot labels in standard classification tasks), minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to minimizing KL divergence.

</details>

**9. How to Set the KL Divergence Coefficient in RL?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

Settings across different papers:
- OpenAI (InstructGPT/PPO-RLHF): KL reward coefficient $\approx 0.01-0.02$
- DPO: Default $\beta = 0.1$; TL;DR experiments $\beta = 0.5$
- DeepSeek (DeepSeekMath/GRPO): KL = 0.04
- Qwen2.5-Math: KL = 0.001

There is no "universal" value; various papers repeatedly emphasize the need to tune the KL coefficient—too small a value allows the model to drift away from the reference model, leading to catastrophic degradation, while too large a value suppresses gains.

</details>
