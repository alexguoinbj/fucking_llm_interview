**1. Differences in Training Process, Pros and Cons of R1 and R1-Zero, and the Value of R1-Zero**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![DeepSeek R1-Zero vs. DeepSeek R1 Pipelines](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig54_differences_in_training_proces.png)




**R1-Zero**:
- Skips the SFT phase directly on the base model (DeepSeek-V3-Base), training purely with large-scale RL (GRPO) + rule-based rewards (such as answer correctness and formatting correctness), without any human-annotated reasoning trajectory data.
- Pros: Proves that long-chain reasoning, self-reflection, self-verification, and other "reasoning" behaviors can spontaneously emerge purely through RL.
- Cons: The generated content suffers from poor readability (language mixing, chaotic formatting, etc.), making it unsuitable for direct deployment as a user-facing product.

**R1**:
- Adopts a multi-stage training pipeline: (1) Cold-start SFT using a small amount of high-quality long CoT data; (2) Large-scale reasoning-oriented RL; (3) A second round of SFT using data generated by the RL-trained model plus additional general-purpose data; (4) Another round of RL oriented toward all scenarios.
- Pros: Balances reasoning capability with output readability, safety, and general conversational ability.
- Cons: The training pipeline is more complex, introducing human annotation/filtering steps.

**The Value of R1-Zero**: It serves as an important scientific validation/proof of concept—demonstrating that with a sufficiently strong base model and suitable reward design, complex reasoning capabilities can completely self-evolve via RL without relying on human-demonstrated reasoning processes.

</details>

**2. Why Might R1-Zero Open a Path for Model Intelligence to Surpass Humans?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

The core logic lies in the fact that the "ceiling" of traditional SFT/RLHF is constrained by the capabilities of human annotators—SFT forces the model to imitate reasoning processes written by humans, and the reward model in RLHF is based on human preference judgments. As a result, it is difficult for models to surpass humans on problems where "even humans do not know how to solve them."

In contrast, the paradigm demonstrated by R1-Zero is that as long as the reward signal is objective and verifiable (such as whether a math problem answer is correct or whether code executes successfully), the model can self-explore, self-verify, and self-improve through large-scale RL to discover reasoning paths that even human annotators have never demonstrated. This is similar to the logic of AlphaZero surpassing all human players through self-play—as long as a task possesses objective reward signals that can be verified at scale, the upper bound of pure RL self-evolution is theoretically unconstrained by the ceiling of human experience.

</details>

**3. Analyzing the Multilingual Language-Mixing Phenomenon in R1-Zero Chain of Thought from a Conceptual Modeling Perspective**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![Dynamic Visualization for Analyzing the Multilingual Language-Mixing Phenomenon in R1-Zero Chain of Thought from a Conceptual Modeling Perspective](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig55_analyzing_the_multilingual_lan.png)




During the reasoning process of R1-Zero, a Chain of Thought (CoT) featuring Chinese-English code-switching emerged, which is an interesting phenomenon worth analyzing deeply from the perspectives of linguistics and model dynamics.

**Phenomenon Description**:

When prompted in Chinese, R1-Zero frequently exhibits Chinese-English code-switching within its internal chain of thought, such as "Let me rethink this problem... Let me check: $2^{10} = 1024$... So the answer should be...", rather than consistently using a single language throughout.

**Explanation from a Conceptual Modeling Perspective**:

- **Activation bias in bilingual representations**: In the pre-training corpus, high-quality content such as mathematical formulas and logical reasoning is far more abundant in English than in Chinese. The model has learned a strong association of "mathematical concepts $\rightarrow$ English token sequences." When reasoning through math problems in its chain of thought, English representations trigger stronger activation for relevant concepts, making them easier to be "retrieved";

- **Incomplete separation of concepts and language**: During the pre-training phase, the representations of abstract concepts (such as "matrix" or "gradient") are not completely independent of their surface language forms; Chinese "matrix" and English "matrix" are not perfectly aligned in the embedding space. During cross-lingual reasoning, concept retrieval may "slip" into the language with stronger pre-training associations;

- **Unconstrained language usage in RL training**: The GRPO training of R1-Zero focuses solely on the correctness of the final answer without imposing any constraints on language consistency within the chain of thought. The model discovered that "mixing Chinese and English during reasoning does not hurt answer accuracy." Lacking gradient signals to correct this language-mixing behavior, the pattern was reinforced and retained;

- **Optimization of information expression efficiency**: From an information-theoretic perspective, the model tends to use the lowest token-cost path to express concepts—certain specialized technical concepts require fewer tokens and are more precise when expressed in English, leading the model to "naturally switch" when encountering them.

**Solution in R1**:

During the SFT phase, R1 incorporates data enforcing language consistency requirements and introduces a language consistency reward in GRPO (penalizing instances where the chain of thought or final answer language disagrees with the input language). This enables the model to learn to maintain monolingual output throughout the chain of thought, eliminating the language-mixing issue present in R1-Zero.

</details>

**5. Why Did DeepSeek R1 Not Use PRM, MCTS, Beam Search, and Other Methods?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

**PRM (Process Reward Model)**:
- It is difficult to precisely define the boundaries of "each reasoning step" in general reasoning tasks.
- Judging whether intermediate steps are correct is inherently difficult, incurring high annotation costs and easily introducing noise.
- PRMs are susceptible to reward hacking—during RL training, the policy model can easily find "shortcut" patterns that trick the PRM into assigning high scores despite low actual reasoning quality.

**MCTS (Monte Carlo Tree Search)**:
- In the token-level generation space of LLMs, the search space explodes exponentially, resulting in an extremely large branching factor.
- Training an accurate value model is inherently difficult, and value estimation errors get amplified during the search process.

**Beam Search**: Tends to generate results that are "locally optimal but lack diversity," which is unhelpful for discovering novel or correct reasoning paths.

Ultimately, the team chose a simpler scheme: rule-based objectively verifiable outcome rewards + GRPO.

</details>

**8. How DeepSeek Distills the Reasoning Capability of R1 into Smaller Models**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

1. Using the trained R1 model to generate high-quality responses containing complete chains of thought for a large set of problems.
2. Using these samples as SFT training data to directly perform supervised fine-tuning on smaller-scale open-source base models.
3. Key finding: Through SFT distillation alone (without requiring additional large-scale RL on the small model), smaller models can achieve reasoning capability improvements significantly superior to what they could attain by directly undergoing RL themselves.

Significance of this result: High-quality reasoning patterns "discovered" by large models through expensive large-scale RL can be efficiently "transferred" to smaller models via simple, low-cost SFT.

**10. STaR Method: Self-Improvement via Self-Generated Reasoning Data**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

**STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner)** core idea is to let the model continuously improve its reasoning capabilities through "self-generated and self-filtered" reasoning data:

1. **Generation phase**: Prompt the current model to generate responses containing both reasoning processes and final answers for each problem.
2. **Filtering phase**: Retain instances where the answer matches ground truth; if the answer is incorrect, perform "rationalization" by providing the ground truth answer post-hoc as a prompt to let the model regenerate the reasoning process.
3. **Fine-tuning phase**: Perform SFT on the model using data obtained from filtering/rationalization.
4. **Iteration**: Repeat the process with the newly fine-tuned model, forming a positive feedback loop.

STaR does not rely on detailed human-annotated reasoning data; it relies solely on relatively easy-to-obtain supervision signals like (problem, final answer) pairs, bootstrapping reasoning capabilities through self-training loops.

</details>

**12. How to Enable Reasoning Models to Automatically Adjust Reasoning Depth Based on Problem Complexity**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

1. **Including samples with varying problem difficulties and corresponding reasonable thinking lengths in training data**: Enabling the model to learn that "thinking length should match problem difficulty."
2. **Introducing "difficulty-aware" length penalties/rewards in the reward function**: Using accuracy from multiple model samplings as a difficulty proxy—imposing stronger length penalties on simple problems and weaker penalties on difficult ones.
3. **Teaching the model to self-evaluate and terminate thinking early**: Training the model to actively generate an end-of-thinking token when it is "sufficiently confident."
4. **Explicit modeling of metacognitive signals**: Having the model first output an estimation of the "required amount of thinking for the problem" before adjusting the depth of subsequent reasoning based on that estimate.

**13. Why the Cost per Output Token for Reasoning Models Is Generally Higher Than for Non-Reasoning Models**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

1. **Hidden "thinking tokens" are included in computation and billing**: Behind a "short answer" seen by the user, there may be hidden token generation that is several to dozens of times the length of that answer.
2. **Larger KV Cache memory footprint**: Longer total sequence lengths mean each request consumes more VRAM, reducing the number of concurrent requests that can be served.
3. **Model size itself may be larger**: Some reasoning models may have larger parameter counts or activated parameters than non-reasoning models of the same generation.
4. **Generation non-determinism and retry costs**: Reasoning tasks sometimes require multiple samplings / self-verifications.

The core factor is the **number of hidden tokens consumed during the thinking process**—which directly translates into extra computational overhead that is "subsidized and amortized" by the final short answer tokens presented to the user.

</details>

**14. How to Enhance Tool-Use Capabilities of LLMs Using RL**

**16. Why Is MLA Better Than MQA Even Though MLA Consumes More KV Cache Than MQA?**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

Compared to MQA, MLA indeed consumes slightly more KV Cache per token, but it is far smaller than standard MHA, and the model quality is significantly superior to MQA.

Reasons why MLA is superior to MQA:

1. **Fundamental expression limit of MQA**: MQA forces all Query heads to share a single set of K and V, resulting in a severe information bottleneck.
2. **Low-rank compression of MLA preserves richer information**: MLA jointly compresses K and V into a low-dimensional latent vector, which is un-projected into distinct K and V for each head via different up-projection matrices during inference—caching a compressed, "information-rich" representation rather than directly discarding information.
3. **MLA achieves a superior trade-off between KV Cache size and model quality**: Exchanging a slightly larger Cache overhead for model quality far exceeding MQA.

</details>

**17. How MLA Solves the Incompatibility Between RoPE and Low-Rank KV**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

**Problem**: RoPE rotational operations and low-rank projections cannot mathematically commute; directly applying RoPE to the compressed low-dimensional latent representation leads to coupling issues.

**MLA's Solution—Decoupled RoPE**: Splitting each token's Key (and corresponding Query) into two parts:

1. **Content part**: Obtained via up-projection from the compressed low-rank latent vector without RoPE applied, carrying semantic information and suitable for compressed caching.
2. **Positional part**: Independently maintaining a smaller-dimensional Key/Query component specifically for RoPE rotation to carry positional information.

The final attention score is obtained by adding the dot products of the two parts: score = (content_q $\cdot$ content_k) + (rope_q $\cdot$ rope_k).

</details>

**18. Why the First 3 Layers of DeepSeek MoE Use Dense Connections Instead of MoE**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

1. **Training stability**: The shallowest layers of the model perform foundational general feature extraction; the routing mechanism of MoE is often unstable in early training, so placing MoE in shallow layers could impair convergence.
2. **Routing quality depends on sufficient contextual/semantic information**: Representations in shallow layers lack rich semantic information, which could degrade routing decision quality. Introducing MoE after several dense layers ensures routing decisions are better informed.
3. **Empirical engineering trade-off**: Using dense layers in shallow levels guarantees foundational capacity and training stability, while using MoE in deeper levels significantly expands model capacity.

</details>

**19. Differences between DeepSeek MoE and Mixtral MoE**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

DeepSeek MoE and Mixtral MoE are two of the most representative MoE architectures at present, exhibiting significant differences in expert granularity, sharing mechanisms, and load balancing strategies.

**1. Expert Granularity**
- **Mixtral (Coarse-Grained)**: Uses a smaller number of large experts (8 experts, activating Top-2 per token). Each expert has a large parameter count, and the division of labor among experts relies on natural emergence through the router.
- **DeepSeek MoE (Fine-Grained)**: Further splits each Mixtral-level large expert into multiple smaller experts (e.g., 64 fine-grained experts, activating Top-6). Through finer division, it achieves more precise knowledge specialization, obtaining richer expert specialization under the same activated parameter count.

**2. Shared Expert Mechanism**
- **Mixtral**: Lacks a dedicated "shared expert" design; all experts compete via the router, and common knowledge is implicitly handled by frequently selected experts.
- **DeepSeek MoE**: Introduces **explicit shared experts (Shared Experts)**—where $K_s$ experts are forcibly activated for all tokens (without routing) to specifically capture common linguistic features. This relieves the burden on routed experts, allowing routed experts to focus more on domain-specific knowledge.

**3. Load Balancing Strategy**
- **Mixtral**: Uses an auxiliary load balancing loss (Auxiliary Loss) to penalize expert load imbalance, which can conflict with the main task loss.
- **DeepSeek-V3**: Introduces an auxiliary-loss-free load balancing strategy—dynamically adjusting expert selection probabilities by adding a bias term to routing logits, eliminating the need for an extra loss term that distorts gradient directions.

**4. Initial Layer Design**

DeepSeek uses dense connections (non-MoE) for the first 3 layers, whereas Mixtral employs MoE starting from the very first layer. The two differ in how they manage training stability.

Overall, DeepSeek MoE achieves more efficient division of knowledge under the same activated parameter count through finer expert granularity and explicit shared experts, representing a systematic improvement over the Mixtral architecture.

</details>

**20. How DeepSeek MoE Expert Load Balancing Resolves Router Collapse**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

Router collapse is a classic issue in MoE model training, referring to the scenario where the router degenerates to always assigning tokens to a few "hot experts," leaving the remaining experts almost never activated.

**Mechanism of Router Collapse**:

In early training, certain experts are randomly initialized slightly better $\rightarrow$ selected more often $\rightarrow$ trained more $\rightarrow$ higher quality $\rightarrow$ router prefers them even more $\rightarrow$ positive feedback loop, ultimately resulting in severe expert load imbalance.

**Traditional Solution: Auxiliary Load Balancing Loss (Auxiliary Loss)**

Adding an auxiliary loss outside the main task loss to penalize unequal expert usage:
\[L_{aux} = \alpha \cdot N \sum_{i=1}^N f_i \cdot P_i\]
**Problem**: The auxiliary loss interferes with the gradient direction of the main task, potentially causing the model to make suboptimal routing decisions purely to balance the load.

**DeepSeek-V3 Improvement: Auxiliary-Loss-Free Load Balancing**

Maintains a dynamic bias term $b_i$ for each expert, adding the bias to the routing logits:
\[Route Score_i = logit_i + b_i\]
- If expert $i$ is over-utilized (load exceeds the target): $b_i$ decreases, making it less likely to be selected;
- If expert $i$ is under-utilized (load is below the target): $b_i$ increases, making it more likely to be selected;
- **Key Point**: The update of $b_i$ does not propagate gradients, leaving the main task loss and gradients unaffected, completely independent of main task optimization.

This design achieves dynamic load balancing among experts without introducing any extra gradient interference, marking a significant advancement in MoE load balancing methods. Meanwhile, DeepSeek prevents extreme cases by dropping tokens when an expert exceeds a specific token threshold (token-dropping), while employing device-level balance constraints to ensure balanced loads across computing nodes.

</details>

**21. Advantages of DeepSeek-V3 Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) Architecture](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig56_advantages_of_deepseek_v3_mult.png)




Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) is a training technique introduced in DeepSeek-V3 that allows the model to predict multiple subsequent tokens simultaneously at each position, rather than the standard "predict only the next token at a time."

**Basic Principle of MTP**:

Standard language model training objective: $\max \sum_t \log P(x_t | x_{<t})$, predicting only $x_t$ at each position. MTP extends this to predict the next $D$ tokens simultaneously at each position via additional prediction heads (Prediction Heads):
\[\max \sum_t \sum_{d=1}^D \lambda_d \log P(x_{t+d} | x_{<t})\]

**Key Advantages**:

- **Denser Training Signals**: Each training sample generates $D$ times the gradient signals, improving data utilization efficiency by $D$ times;
- **Better Long-Range Dependency Modeling**: Training the model to attend to multi-step future information encourages capturing longer-range semantic dependencies, enhancing overall comprehension of text structure;
- **Accelerated Inference (with Speculative Decoding)**: The additional prediction heads in MTP can be directly leveraged for speculative decoding—using small prediction heads to quickly draft multiple candidate tokens and then validating them with the main model, theoretically enabling up to nearly $D\times$ inference speedup (depending in practice on token acceptance rates);
- **Architecture Agnosticism**: MTP is an improvement purely at the training objective level, leaving the backbone architecture during inference unchanged, which allows seamless integration with other technologies such as MoE and MLA;
- **Enhanced Performance on Structured Tasks**: In tasks like code and mathematics, dependencies between adjacent tokens exhibit regular patterns. MTP is particularly effective for learning such long-range patterns, yielding especially pronounced improvements for DeepSeek-V3 on code generation tasks.

**Note**: MTP increases computational cost during training (calculating loss for multiple prediction heads) and requires appropriately setting loss weights $\lambda_d$ for each prediction head to prevent secondary prediction objectives from disturbing the optimization direction of the main task.

</details>

**22. Matrix Computations Utilizing FP8 Quantization in DeepSeek-V3 Mixed-Precision Training**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

DeepSeek-V3 introduces FP8 mixed-precision during training, making it one of the first works to successfully apply FP8 in large-scale LLM training. The core challenge lies in maximizing FP8 computational efficiency while maintaining numerical stability.

**Computations Using FP8 (Computation-Intensive Parts)**:

- **Feed-Forward Network (FFN) Matrix Multiplications**: GEMM operations of the weight matrices $W_1$, $W_2$, and $W_3$ and their corresponding activation values in FFNs, which constitute the largest computational workload of the entire model; FP8 saves approximately 50% of GPU memory bandwidth;
- **Attention Mechanism Projection Matrices**: Matrix multiplications of attention projections such as $W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$, and $W_O$;
- **MoE Expert Layer Matrix Computations**: FFN matrix multiplications of each expert, which yield particularly significant benefits in MoE models (due to the large number of experts, saving a substantial amount of memory).

**Computations Retaining BF16/FP32 (Precision-Sensitive Parts)**:

- **Master Weights**: Parameters are stored and updated in BF16 or FP32 formats, while FP8 is used only for intermediate matrix multiplication computations during forward and backward passes;
- **Gradient Accumulation and Optimizer States**: Momentum and variance terms of Adam are maintained in FP32;
- **Normalization Layers (RMSNorm)**: Kept in BF16 due to small dynamic range and high precision sensitivity;
- **Residual Connections**: Kept in BF16 to prevent error accumulation from precision loss;
- **Loss Computation**: Maintained in FP32 to ensure accurate gradient signals.

**Key Technologies for FP8 Precision Management**:
- **Fine-Grained Quantization (Tile-wise Scaling)**: Independently computes scaling factors for each small block (tile, e.g., $128 \times 128$) of the matrix rather than sharing one across the entire matrix, significantly reducing quantization error;
- **Delayed Quantization**: Tracks historical distributions of activation values using a sliding window to stabilize quantization scaling factors, avoiding quantization failures caused by single-step outliers.

</details>

**23. Advantages of DualPipe Parallel Training Algorithm Over Traditional Pipeline Parallelism**

<details>
<summary>💡 Show Answer</summary>

![DualPipe Parallel Pipeline Scheduling](https://img.pricenode.uk/llm_interview_questions/english/chapter5/fig57_advantages_of_dualpipe_paralle.png)




DualPipe is a novel pipeline parallelism algorithm proposed by DeepSeek-V3 that systematically addresses efficiency bottlenecks present in traditional pipeline parallelism for large MoE model training.

**Core Issue of Traditional Pipeline Parallelism—Pipeline Bubble**:

In standard 1F1B (One Forward One Backward) pipelines, forward and backward passes must execute sequentially. The bubble ratio is approximately $(p-1)/(m+p-1)$ (where $p$ is the number of pipeline stages and $m$ is the number of micro-batches). Deeper pipelines result in higher bubble ratios and lower effective GPU utilization.

**Core Innovation of DualPipe—Bidirectional Overlapping Scheduling**:

DualPipe schedules forward and backward passes in a **bidirectionally overlapped execution**:
- Traditional approach: Forward phase $\rightarrow$ Waiting $\rightarrow$ Backward phase (sequential, prominent bubbles);
- DualPipe: Simultaneously schedules forward and backward computations across different pipeline parts so that they **mutually fill each other's bubbles** in time, significantly reducing GPU idle time.

**Specific Advantages Over Traditional Schemes**:

1. **Lower Bubble Ratio**: Through bidirectional scheduling, the bubble ratio is reduced by approximately 50%, increasing GPU utilization from 75--85% to over 90%;

2. **Overlap with MoE All-to-All Communication**: Forward pass in MoE models requires cross-GPU expert communication. DualPipe can run communication operations in **parallel with computation** of other layers (computation-communication overlap), further eliminating communication wait latency;

3. **Support for Deeper Pipeline Depths**: With the bubble problem mitigated, more pipeline stages can be utilized, enabling better scaling to ultra-large-scale training;

4. **Reduced Dependence on Micro-Batch Count**: Traditional methods require a large number of micro-batches to dilute the bubble fraction. DualPipe maintains high efficiency even with smaller micro-batch counts, making it better suited for modern large-batch training scenarios.

**Cost**: DualPipe needs to schedule intermediate activations for both forward and backward passes simultaneously, which increases peak GPU memory consumption and requires integration with activation checkpointing to control memory usage.

</details>
